My Nordic Adventure

A research visit to NORDITA mixed with travel through Sweden and Norway

Read the full post

I just got back from an extended nordic vacation where I visited Stockholm, Gothenburg, and Oslo. This was all on the back of an invitation I received from a collaborator to visit the Nordic Institute of Theoretical Physics (NORDITA) so we could push a couple of the projects we have going on currently. I of course also used the visit as an opportunity to make new connections and to scout the place for a potential postdoc in the coming years.

It was my first time visiting Scandinavia, which has been on my bucket list for a long time, and I was particularly impressed by the laid-back atmosphere of Sweden and Norway. The people I encountered were all very friendly and there was a real sense of life enjoyement among them (possibly due to the saunas littered everywhere). All three of the cities I visited had ample green space and major waterfronts, which was reminiscent of my hometown Toronto that resides on one of the great lakes: lake Ontario. They also had 7-eleven stores everywhere - another after school and late night delicacy that I grew up with in Canada - which seems to be uniquely a Scandinavian thing as far as Europe is concerned since I have not been able to find them anywhere else in my travels throughout Europe. To savour the moment I bought a 7-eleven sweater that I probably do not need but nostalgia is a powerful force let me tell you.

Stockholm in particular is like this lake archipelago sandwiched between two large land masses, with each area having its own charming personality. Upon my initial arrival in Stockholm, I explored the Norrmalm district, which is where the central train station T-centralen is located. One thing that immediately stands out to you if you take the metro is just how unique some of the stations are, with huge custom artworks by Swedish artists covering the walls. I went up and down the famous Drottninggaten street with many interesting shops, stopping to buy some local Swedish chocolate; to the city hall to have a nice portside view of Stockholm's old town, called Gamla Stan; and strolled through the King's garden (in fact many gardens were strolled through that day). I was also inspired by the artworks I had seen earlier to visit the National Museum as well as the Moderna Museet and the rest of the Skeppsholmen island that is sandwiched between the old town and one of the larger islands where Vasa ship wreck is housed. The National Museum had a beautiful marble staircase that led into a grand multi-arched room littered with marble statues and a central fresco depicting some sort of religious scene. They had an interesting exhibit on a Swedish royal named Gustav Badin, who was a former African slave that made his way to the top - very inspiring stuff. Skeppsholmen island is packed full of character for such a small space, with a strong nautical influence and multiple hillside forts offering scenic views of the the surrounding islands, and the supposedly beloved Gröna Lund which is an amusement park in the middle of Stockholm. The island also has an artistic streak with the famous Moderna Museet, which has an unusual but striking abstract sculpture garden just outside of it. The museum itself had a lot of experimental contemporary pieces mixed in with surrealist paintings, and I was very fortunate that they had on a Picasso exhibit showcasing some of his earlier cubist work which was splendid (as usual). I managed to also visit the Medelhavsmuseet where I saw some Egyptian and Greek artifacts, and bought a pair of smart-looking hieroglyphic socks (I love socks by the way).

The next day (with great enthusiasm) I went to the island containing the Vasa shipwreck. This island is massive with many places I wanted to see and so little time.

The day after, I embarked on a morning cruise around some of the islands in the company of a charming local with a dry sense of humour, who happened to be our tour guide; it was a great day for a cruise with clear, sunny weather. They took us near the outskirts of Stockholm, where there were many small islands with bathing spots and cabins to relax in, and so close to the city centre too. I then took the metro to the opposite end of Stockholm to tour the UNESCO world heritage site Drottningholm palace, which is a residence of the Swedish royal family. The palace sits at the edge of the water and the palace interior is lavish (like all palaces I suppose) with brilliant mythological frescos that have a golden glow from the lighting. The palace gardens are massive compared to the palace, and I tried my best to assail them. I first went through the lake area that has small islands with isolated statues on them, then swung around to the gothic tower that lies just beyond them and had a solemnly walk through open fields where I felt in touch with the world. After my little moment I went by a nice gatehouse for the palace and then arrived at the Chinese pavilion, which was an elegant villa with asian-inspired interior and two arms giving a fantastic view over the entire palace gardens. I also stopped by the former studio of the Swedish painter Evert Lundquist - an important figure in modern Swedish painting, which was unfortunately closed until summer, but I peered through the windows to see a studio left untouched with original artworks on display. I promptly made my way through the rest of the gardens and then hopped on the metro again, where I got off around the Bromma area. I was about to head off to the city centre again when my appetite got the better of me and I stopped by this Pakistani foodtruck called Tajis foodtuck. Man that was some of the most delicious food I have ever eaten, plus I got a mango drink imported from Pakistan to wash it all down. With my stomach full, I again crossed the city to the Djurgårdens. with Prince Eugens art gallery, then parts of Gamla Stan.

The final day before starting my research stay, I ventured back to Gamla Stan to see the rest of it and also Södermalm.

NORDITA sits in the heart of the University of Stockholm campus, with a bridge linking it to the rest of the physics department. During my stay there, I was introduced to some interesting researchers working on topics ranging from quantum gravity to sphere packing. In fact, one of the researchers working on sphere packing is the grandson of one of my grandmother's closest friends; what a small world! We enjoyed a few donuts while we chatted for the first time, about family, research, and cool things to do in Stockholm. On one of the days the well known experimentalist from IQOQI Wien - Markus Aspelmeyer - was visiting the university and my collaborator had scheduled a meeting with him, along with a few other people from the group of Magdalena Zych, to talk about some of our research. It was fascinating listening to everyone there.

The main project that my collaborator and I worked on was this idea of getting a reinforcement learning agent to perform quantum information tasks that were implicitly embedded in spacetime, and have the structure of spacetime appear in the agent's memory after training. I wont go into the complexity associated with the term "agent," but you can roughly think of it as something embedded in a world that can interact and influence this world, and which has the capability to make choices concerning its actions. This agent we envision has no prior understanding of what spacetime is nor do they see the connection to the abstract quantum information task: they start out blindly exploring, eventually mastering the task by building a representation of the spacetime structure that the environment is embedded in. The other project we worked on was related to contextuality in quantum field theory. I also delivered a talk that week on my prior work titled "Universal Quantum Computer from Relativistic Motion," which essentially shows that given the right conditions, one can assemble a universal set of quantum gates from the interaction of a number of qubits moving through spacetime and a relativistic quantum field. The talk went well (at least I thought so) and there was quite a bit of engagement from the audience. Many of the audience members joined for the lunch afterwards, where I had some really stimulating conversations on the philosophy of quantum gravity and spacetime emergence.

Outside of work I spent most of my time hanging out with some of the master's and PhD students from NORDITA. We spent many hours discussing and debating in pubs, the complexities of the world, and shared many laughs together too. On the last evening we went to the pub we frequented the most and my collaborator - who was by this point best friends with one of the bartenders - got them to give us a table in the back despite the place being packed with students. It turns out that the table was directly across from a big private retirement (or maybe it was a birthday?) celebration, which was slightly awkward given that we were the only people not part of it. My collaborators boss also joined us that evening for a few beers and we had some interesting conversations about AI and spacetime emergence.

I also managed to visit the Stockholm opera house one evening to catch a performance of Madama Butterfly by Puccini, which is based on a short story and originally premiered in New York in 1900. The opera house is located just across the bridge from the parliament building, in a lovely little square called Gustav Adolfs torg. I arrived just on time for the performance, wearing my opera outfit of a grey blazer with a white dress shirt and navy blue dress pants that I have worn to every opera I have ever seen (it is tradition now), but I got lost initially and one of the staff had to escort me to my seat. The interior was quite lavish with a gorgeous ceiling and grand chandelier, and the performance was fantastic. I knew Madama Butterfly was a Puccini opera - quite a famous one at that - so it was bound to be excellent. Indeed it was, and I had not read the plot synopsis beforehand, so I was surprised to find the opera in the modern setting of early 1900s Japan. The opera is in three acts (the premiere had only two acts but due to poor reception, Puccini decided to split the last act into two) and follows the story of a young girl named Cio-Cio-San, known affectionately as "Butterfly," and a US naval officer named Pinkerton. It starts out with Pinkerton visiting Nagasaki to marry Butterfly for convenience because he plans to leave her once he finds an American wife, and since Japanese divorce laws are not strict. They have their ceremony and first night together, then Pinkerton leaves to America for many years. The second act is essentially Butterfly waiting for Pinkerton to return while rejecting all attempts by her maids and townspeople to marry her to another person. It culminates with Pinkerton's promise to return after the reveal that Butterfly gave birth to his son. Once Pinkerton has arrived with his new wife to retrieve the child, the enthusiasm of Butterfly makes him regret his decisions and the ensuing chaos results in Butterfly taking her own life.

After my research visit in Stockholm I took the train across southern Sweden to Gothenburg and stayed there for about two days. Although not as vibrant a metropolis as Stockholm, Gothenburg still has some interesting history and spots. I particularly enjoyed my wanderings through the old town - stopping at some beautiful churches, the lovely Gothenburg cathedral, some upscale markets, and the park that wraps around the perimeter - as well as my journey down Kungsportsavenyen to the Gothenburg Museum of Art and my visit to the Haga district; Oh, and I cannot forgot the cruise I took to the archipelago that sits just outside the main river that Gothenburg is situated by. It was a somewhat rainy day when I visited the Gothenburg Museum of Art, making it the perfect opportunity to stroll through the gallery. They had a small but impressive collection of impressionist paintings as well as some nice Picasso's - what a great way to spend a rainy afternoon. The Haga district was such a vibrant and hip place full of edgy university students, with all sorts of niche shops and vintage/antique stores, and the vibes at the coffee shops were unbeatable; I ordered an iced pistachio coffee that was tres magnifique. The archipelago just outside of Gothenburg hosts many fishing settlements and weekend getaway spots for swimming, and the cruise took us all around them. The tour guide gave us a bit of history while sailing and I learned more about the turbulent history between Sweden and Denmark, with the guide mentioning that Gothenburg was captured by the Danes and held for ransom for an absurd amount of money - which Sweden actually ended up paying!

I then travelled to my final destination - Oslo - after my short sojourn in Gothenburg. Of all the places I visited during this trip, Oslo was my absolute favourite! It has the same relaxed lakeside atmosphere as Stockholm (and just as many 7-elevens), but with a stronger artistic presence due to being the home of Edvard Munch and beautiful fjords leading into the harbour, plus some colourful small fishing houses dotting the many islands near the harbour.
