Weekend in Milano

A weekend of art galleries and opera at the legendary La Scala opera theatre

Read the full post

My love of opera is a somewhat recent development that began shortly into my master's degree at Waterloo. It evolved principally from my admiration of classical music, which I was getting more into as a means for concentrating background music. Ironically, a lot of opera melodies and arias are quite striking and not the greatest background companions but I grew to really appreciate the technical aspects of the craft; not to mention that some of the most iconic pieces ever written were created for operas. Pieces such as Nessun Dorma by Giacomo Puccini, Largo al factotum della citta by Gioachino Rossini, or L'amour est un oiseau rebelle by Georges Bizet are firmly embedded in the public conciousness.

The first opera I went to see was Carmen by Bizet, which was at the Four Seasons Theatre in downtown Toronto and was performed by the Canadian Opera Company. Carmen was (and is still) a magnificent opera with many famous pieces such as L'amour est un oiseau rebelle and Toreador's Song. It is one of the best operas you can see as an introduction to opera due to its sweeping sets and grand soundtrack, and from that moment on I was hooked. I immediately drafted up a list (as I usually do) of operas and opera houses I wanted to see, and started to regularly attend operas wherever I could.

Moving to Innsbruck to pursue my PhD in physics was essentially a gift from the opera gods. Opera traces its history back to a group of intellectuals called the Florentine Camerata from 16th century Firenze, Italy, who wanted to revive the musical and dramatic theatre of the ancient Greeks. Opera became extremely popular and spread to all corners of Europe during and after the renaissance. As you can imagine then, opera is endemic in Europe with virtually every city possessing an opera house and some of the most famous theatres being present there. Probably the most prestigious of those theatres is Teatro alla Scala in Milano, which brings me to the story I wanted to tell.

I had been dying to visit Teatro alla Scala ever since moving to Europe but I either already had plans or could not find any tickets. Due to its status, tickets sell out very fast - often in just a couple days after going on sale - so last November I chose an Italian opera and marked down the date when the tickets first went on sale; I was determined to get there. I almost didn't get any tickets but I managed to snag some cheaper seats in a booth next to the stage. The opera I chose was Turandot by Puccini, which actually debuted at Teatro alla Scala on April 25th 1926, making this the 100th anniversary performance. While he completed the final scene of Turandot, the original score was never completed by Puccini himself, who perished near the end of it. The score was only finished a year or so later by the composer Franco Alfano. Turandot is set in China and details the attempted courtship of the cold-hearted princess Turandot by prince Calaf. Turandot's father wants to marry her off to someone worthy, so a challenge is issued to potential suitors: answer three riddles correctly to gain my daughters hand in marriage, but get any of them wrong and you shall be executed. Many suitors perish but the prince Calaf manages to overcome the challenge, however, the princess refuses to marry him. The prince offers her a way out: guess my name correctly by dawn and I shall accept execution. For the rest you will have to go and see the opera yourself!

The interior of La Scala was phenomenal. The grand neoclassical design by architect Giuseppe Piermarini makes you feel like you are an Italian noble - perhaps a Medici family member - visiting the palace of a Lombard king. The foyer - known as the Toscanini Foyer - is packed with statues of famous opera composers such as Giuseppe Verdi and Gioachino Rossini, inundating the atmosphere with a sense of historical importance. The auditorium and stage are very opulent, with incredible acoustics to boot. I spent some moments before and during the performance just basking in the sheer beauty of the space. While my view of the stage was somewhat obscured, I sat behind a lovely Dutch couple who had recently moved to Milano, and they let me peer over them during the performance.

Turandot is one of the most popular operas for a reason, and the production I saw that evening was a masterpiece. The stage design was brilliant, having many dynamic scenes from Chinese streets to imperial interiors. The singers were some of the best I have ever witnessed, especially the commanding voice of prince Calaf and the sweeter voice of Liu. When it came time for prince Calaf to sing the aria Nessun Dorma, I was overwhelmed with such raw emotion that I could not help but shed some tears - what a performance!

Outside of the opera I made my way to many different art galleries in Milano, seeing more experimental art at Pirelli Hangar Bicocca (a former factory) and medieval/renaissance art at Poldi Pezzoli museum. I was particularly impressed with the collection at Museo del Novecento, which had some great futurist paintings by Boccioni. Likewise, the formerly private collection of the collectors Antonio Boschi and Marieda Di Stefano in their charming apartment - now Casa Museo Boschi Di Stefano - was quite spectacular, and I was captivated by how diverse the set of compositions were. I of course also made the somewhat expensive pilgrimage to see the Last Supper by da Vinci, which was made even more sublime thanks to the careful explanation of my tour guide. Part of the genius of da Vinci with the Last Supper was to capture the moment when Jesus reveals to his apostles that one of them will betray him, creating this tension in the painting and allowing him to depict a range of different emotions, which broke from centuries of tradition depicting the moment after. The painting was so revolutionary that subsequent iterations of the Last Supper by other artists almost exclusively depicted da Vinci's motifs.

I also had a cup of coffee at the Wes Anderson designed retro diner in the Fondazione Prada and visited the Santuario di San Bernardino alle Ossa, which is a famous ossuary containing walls filled with human skulls.
